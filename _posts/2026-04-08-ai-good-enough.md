Look, I get it. You've been hearing about the AI revolution for years now. It was supposed to take all our jobs, restructure the economy, and either usher in a golden age of prosperity or destroy civilization as we know it. And yet...here you are. Still employed. Still ordering from the same streaming services. Still doing your taxes by hand like some kind of medieval peasant.

The hype machine promised us the future, and what we got was a chatbot that confidently hallucinates about the French Revolution and a bunch of tech CEOs on stage saying "paradigm shift" with a straight face. Companies threw billions at AI initiatives, and the vast majority of them failed. Spectacularly. There are published case studies of these failures, and frankly, reading them is like watching someone try to put out a grease fire with a garden hose while their employees stand around filling out adoption surveys.

Most people still aren't using AI in any meaningful way. The economy hasn't restructured. Jobs haven't disappeared. Daily life looks basically the same. And the public discourse about AI? It's about shitty image generation and AI datacenters wasting obscene amounts of water. Meanwhile, the most transformative technology in human history is being judged by its dumbest applications.

So the narrative writes itself: AI was overpromised. It's not as powerful as they said. We can all relax.

I think this is completely, dangerously wrong. And I'd like to explain why.

## The Packaging Problem

Here's the thing about those corporate AI failures. I've read the case studies. The implementation efforts were, and I cannot stress this enough, *embarrassingly* bad. We're talking about companies that chose substandard models, rolled them out with garbage communication, measured success by whether *everyone in the company* was using the tool, and then threw up their hands when Karen from accounting didn't use ChatGPT to revolutionize her spreadsheet workflow.

This is not evidence that AI doesn't work. This is evidence that most organizations are bad at adopting new technology. Which, if you've ever watched your company attempt literally anything new, should not surprise you in the slightest.

The gap between what AI can do and what most people are doing with it is not a technology problem. It's a packaging problem. The reason your neighbor isn't using AI to automate half their job isn't because the AI isn't good enough — it's because nobody has put it in front of them in a way that makes them realize how stupidly easy it is. The advertising hasn't caught up. The interfaces haven't caught up. The cultural understanding hasn't caught up.

And honestly? Most people aren't even using the *basic* stuff right. The average person's experience with AI is typing a question into ChatGPT and getting a mediocre answer. That's like judging the internet by AskJeeves. You know what you should actually be doing with AI for email? Feed it every email you've ever written, have it build a style guide of your voice, and then have it draft from that. But nobody does this, because nobody knows they can.

But the capability? The capability is already there. And I can prove it.

## What Happened Last Tuesday

I have an app on my phone. I hold the power button, it starts listening, I say what I want, and it sends my instructions to an AI agent on my computer that writes the code. I'm not sitting at a desk programming. I'm talking to my phone while making dinner.

Last Tuesday I told it: "Hey, scrape all the courses off Skillshare. Here's the link." A few minutes later it was done. Then I said: "Build me a website so I can browse these." I didn't describe the website. Didn't spec a layout, didn't mention colors or fonts or navigation. I just said "build me a website." Minutes later, there's a website.

And then I stopped. Because somewhere between "I should download this one course before my trial ends" and "I have their entire site," I realized I should probably ask myself whether I *should* be doing this. Maybe I should just pay for Skillshare like a normal person.

That's the part I want you to sit with. Not the ethics of scraping — the *speed*. I had a casual thought, the kind of thing you might think in the shower and forget by breakfast. And in less time than it takes to watch a single episode of television, that casual thought had become a fully functional system that had downloaded an entire platform's content library. I went from "maybe I'll grab one course" to "I have everything" before I'd even had time to think about whether I should.

## This Isn't a One-Off

If the Skillshare story were the only example, you could write it off as a cute anecdote. But it's not. It's not even close. Here's what the last six months of my life have looked like:

I didn't like any of the note-taking apps on the market, so over a weekend I built a personalized one. It's a full Android app with voice recognition that syncs to GitHub, where an AI agent automatically organizes my notes into categories. It's got OAuth, lock-screen capture, the works. A weekend.

I wanted to control my camera gimbal remotely. I'm a mechanical engineer — I know nothing about electronics. So I had AI help me use an oscilloscope to reverse engineer the gimbal's serial protocol, told me what hardware to buy, wrote the firmware for an ESP32 board, and built a web app to control the whole thing over Wi-Fi. I now have a motorized camera slider with keyframe animation and spline interpolation that I control from a browser. A mechanical engineer. Doing electronics engineering. Because AI told me how.

I wanted better book recommendations. I have a couple hundred reviews on Goodreads, years of opinions about what I love and hate. I told AI to read all of them and build a profile of my taste. Then I pointed it at a Reddit thread with 4,000 book recommendations and said "scrape Goodreads reviews for all of these and tell me which ones I'd actually like." Since I started reading AI-recommended books instead of picking my own, my average rating has gone from 3.6 to 4.2. The machine literally knows my taste in books better than I do.

I casually built a website to compare image compression formats because I was curious whether AVIF was worth it over WebP. I built a password strength calculator that simulates every known attack strategy in parallel. I built a tool that identifies which of the six editions of Darwin's *Origin of Species* a text snippet comes from. I downloaded Onshape's entire documentation and built myself a personalized CAD support agent. I did the same for Premiere Pro.

I used to post every project on my blog, because building things was exciting and rare enough to be worth writing about. I haven't posted in months. Not because I stopped building — because I'm building things so fast I can't keep up with *writing about them*.

And it's not just me. A good friend of mine with zero software engineering background spent a month and built the food recommendation app she'd always dreamed of. Another friend built an entire website for Bagh Chal — a niche Nepali board game that has never had a decent online version — in an afternoon.

And do you think I sat down and carefully recalled all of these projects for you? Of course not. I asked Claude on my phone to go through every repo on my computer from the past six months and make a compilation of the coolest stuff. Even the act of *listing what AI built for me* was done by AI.

We live in an era where your mom can build whatever software she wants. The prerequisite is not skill. It's not money. It's not education. It's *desire*. And that is a fundamentally new thing in the world.

## "But That's Just Piracy"

When I made the Skillshare argument to my friend Luke, he had a pretty reasonable response: the same gap already exists for piracy. You can already pirate basically anything if you try hard enough, and most people don't bother because of skill, convenience, or just not wanting to feel like a criminal.

Fair point. But here's where I think this is qualitatively different.

Traditional piracy required you to know what a torrent client was, how to find reliable trackers, how to avoid malware, and how to not get a scary letter from your ISP. That's a real skill barrier. It's been sitting there for 20 years, and it's never gone away, because the tooling stayed roughly the same difficulty level the entire time.

AI-assisted "I'm going to build the exact tool I need in 45 minutes" has no such stable floor. The tools are getting more accessible every month. They're being packaged into consumer products. Your mom is going to have one on her phone. This isn't like learning to torrent; it's like learning to Google. The barrier is collapsing toward zero, and it's not going to stop.

And here's the asymmetry that keeps me up at night: a streaming service has maybe a couple dozen engineers working on DRM and anti-scraping measures. The world has *billions* of people who want their content. Every defensive measure is a moving target, and you're being chased by a planet. Defense was already losing this war when the only weapon was BitTorrent. Now every person on earth is about to get their own personal software engineer.

The power imbalance is vastly in favor of the population, not the company.

Now, I do want to acknowledge something, because I think it's important. I have active Patreon accounts where I'm paying money to creators whose entire body of work I could download for free. I think there absolutely is room for people who just want to support the things they love. But we have to be honest about what's happening: business models built on access control and artificial scarcity are living on borrowed time. The digital content economy is going to restructure whether we're ready for it or not.

## The Force Multiplier

But let's zoom out from content piracy, because that's actually the *least* scary part of this argument.

Here's what I think most people are getting wrong about why corporate AI initiatives fail. They look at a company of 1,000 people where AI adoption flopped, and they think the AI wasn't good enough. What I think actually happened is that maybe 5 out of those 1,000 people had the creativity and initiative to genuinely leverage AI, and the company measured success by whether the other 995 were using it too. Of course it looked like a failure. You measured the wrong thing.

This sounds like a niche observation about corporate metrics. It is not. It is the entire ballgame.

Throughout all of human history, there has been a hard cap on individual output. Even if you were the most brilliant, creative, hardworking person on the planet, you could only do as much work as your personal time and effort allowed. A genius could outperform their peers by what, 5x? 10x? Whatever the number, it was bounded by the number of hours in a day and the number of hands on their body.

AI obliterates this constraint.

If you have a company of 1,000 people and one of them truly understands how to use AI, that person is eventually going to get to the point where they can spawn a thousand AI agents. It won't matter that only 0.1% of the company figured it out. That 0.1% can now scale their output to match — or exceed — the rest of the company combined.

The old world capped individual output. The new world does not. And I don't think people have wrapped their heads around what that means.

## The Wrong Metric for Danger

This is the thing that I've been dancing around, and I think it's time to just say it plainly.

When people talk about whether AI is dangerous, they're almost always looking at *general adoption*. They look at the aggregate: are most people using it effectively? Are most companies seeing returns? Is the economy restructuring? And when the answers come back "no, not really," they feel reassured.

This is the wrong metric. It is catastrophically, almost comically the wrong metric.

General adoption does not matter. It has never mattered. If 7 billion people fail to effectively use AI but one single hacker figures it out, that hacker can still ruin the world.

We don't measure the danger of nuclear weapons by asking whether the average country has successfully integrated them into their military strategy. We don't measure the danger of bioweapons by polling laboratories on their average adoption of gain-of-function research. The question was never "can the average person wield this?" The question is "can the *most dangerous* person wield this?"

And the answer is already yes.

Recently, Anthropic built a coding model so powerful that they decided not to release it to the public. Instead, they did a closed release to major software companies, so those companies could patch their security vulnerabilities before the general public got access to a tool that could find and exploit them. When I shared this with friends, the response was exactly what you'd expect: "Yeah, sure, but AI companies always say their stuff is great."

That reaction — that reflexive dismissal — is exactly what I've been talking about this entire essay. The "AI underwhelm" narrative has become so dominant that people now dismiss concrete, specific danger signals because they've been primed to believe AI is all hype. The boy who cried wolf is a cautionary tale about a boy. But the wolf was real.

## The Prophet of Doom Problem

I realize that I am, at this point, essentially arguing for a very specific position: that AI is already good enough to cause catastrophic harm, and that the evidence people cite to the contrary — the failed corporate initiatives, the lack of visible economic restructuring, the general vibe of "meh" — is not just irrelevant but actively misleading.

The reason I think it's important to be loud about this is that the popular narrative has a very specific failure mode. When people hear that AI has "big predictions but hasn't seemed to fulfill them," their default assumption is that the AI isn't good enough. When they see a company try to adopt AI and fail, they conclude there was something wrong with the technology.

"If AI is so terrifying and so world-changing, then why hasn't it been transforming these companies that tried to adopt it?"

Because *those companies* are not the threat model. The threat model is not the average. The threat model is the tail. It's the one person out of a thousand who actually understands what they're holding. And thanks to AI, that one person is no longer limited by their own two hands.

AI is already good enough to ruin the world. We're just waiting on tooling, infrastructure, and adoption to catch up. And those are packaging problems.

Packaging problems get solved.
