Every time a major technology has displaced workers, we tell ourselves the same bedtime story. The looms put weavers out of work, but it created jobs in textile factories. The automobile killed the farrier, but it created mechanics. The computer eliminated typing pools, but it created IT departments. New technology destroys old jobs and creates new ones. This is Jevon's Paradox, it's been true for centuries, and it's the reason you shouldn't worry about AI.

I think this time, the story is wrong. And I think understanding *why* it's wrong is one of the most important things you can do right now.

If you haven't read [the companion piece](link-to-capabilities-essay), the short version is this: AI is already capable of extraordinary things, and the reason it hasn't visibly transformed the world yet is a packaging problem, not a technology problem. That essay is about what AI can do right now. This one is about what happens when everyone figures that out.

## The Horse Problem

Let me tell you about horses.

In 1900, there were 21 million horses in America serving a population of 76 million people. That's roughly one horse for every four humans. Horses were *the* technology. They plowed fields, hauled freight, carried people, powered industry. If you wanted to move anything — yourself, your goods, your economy — you needed a horse.

Then the car showed up. And the tractor. And the truck.

By 2000, there were about 6 million horses serving a population of 336 million. One horse for every fifty people. A 92% collapse in the horse-to-human ratio. The horse economy didn't transform. It didn't "create new jobs for horses." It evaporated.

Now, I know what you're thinking. *We're not horses, Carson. We're the humans in this story. We're the ones who invented the car.*

I don't think so. Not this time.

## This Time Is Actually Different

I know, I know. "This time is different" is famously the most dangerous phrase in economics. But hear me out, because the thing that made every previous technological displacement survivable was two characteristics: it was **slow** and it was **partial**.

The automobile took *decades* to fully replace the horse. Decades in which farriers could retrain, in which the workforce could adjust, in which new industries could grow organically to absorb displaced workers. And it was partial — it replaced a specific function (transportation and hauling) performed by a specific part of the workforce. It didn't touch doctors, teachers, accountants, writers, or lawyers.

AI is neither slow nor partial.

Every previous automation replaced a *task*. Looms replaced weaving. Calculators replaced arithmetic. Assembly robots replaced specific motions on a factory floor. The human still had other capabilities. The loom couldn't do your taxes. The calculator couldn't write your legal brief. The robot arm couldn't manage your team.

AI is not replacing a task. It is replacing *every cognitive capability that the entire human is capable of performing*. Writing, analyzing, coding, designing, strategizing, researching, communicating, creating — there is no cognitive function where AI is not either already competitive or rapidly approaching it.

This is what makes the "new jobs will be created" argument break down. New jobs for humans require humans to have some capability that the replacement technology doesn't. When the car replaced the horse for transportation, humans could say "well, horses can't operate a gas station." When AI replaces humans for *thinking*, what exactly is the new job that requires a capability AI doesn't have?

## "But It Can't Replace a Plumber"

This is the best counterargument, and I want to take it seriously. AI can't snake a drain. It can't frame a wall. It can't do surgery, pour concrete, or rewire a house. Physical labor is safe, at least for now, and maybe for a long time. Fine. I'll grant the entire premise. Every single blue-collar, physical-labor job survives AI completely intact.

It doesn't matter.

If the entire white-collar middle class loses their jobs — or even a large fraction of them — they don't have money. And if they don't have money, they can't pay the plumber. The electrician. The contractor. The surgeon. The plumber doesn't lose his job because AI replaced him. He loses his job because his customers went broke.

The economy is a circle, not a line. You can't nuke half of it and expect the other half to keep spinning.

## It Doesn't Have to Be Everyone

Here's the thing that I think people really underestimate. When I make these arguments, the response is usually some version of "well, maybe, but AI isn't going to take *my* job." And maybe that's true. Maybe you're safe.

AI doesn't need to take your job. It doesn't need to take everyone's job. It needs to take maybe 20 or 30 percent of jobs, and the world as we know it comes apart.

Think about it concretely. Let's say you're an accountant at a mid-size firm with 30 people. AI gets good enough that the best 20 accountants can now do the work that used to require 30. Your firm doesn't need to fire everyone. It just fires 10 people. That's a third of the workforce, and the firm is *thriving* — more efficient than ever. Great news for the company. Catastrophic news for those 10 people.

Now where do those 10 accountants go?

They can't go be teachers, because schools just figured out the same math and cut a third of *their* staff. They can't go be software engineers, because tech companies cut even harder. They can't go be marketing professionals, because — you see where this is going. Every single industry is doing the same optimization at the same time. There is no adjacent field to absorb the displaced workers, because *every* field is contracting simultaneously.

And here's the really cruel part: the people who keep their jobs will be the ones who are best at using AI. The people who get fired will be the ones who are worst at adopting it. In the new economy, the critical skill — the one thing that determines whether you have a paycheck or not — is the ability to effectively leverage AI. Which is, by definition, the exact skill that the displaced workers do not have. Because if they had it, they wouldn't be the ones who got fired.

So when someone says "AI will create new jobs for these people," ask yourself: new jobs doing *what*? The new economy rewards AI fluency. These are the people who just demonstrated they don't have it. And unlike previous technological shifts, there isn't a 20-year runway to retrain. This is happening in years, not decades.

## The Cascade

Let's follow the dominoes.

Thirty percent of the workforce is displaced. These people — tens of millions of them in America alone, potentially billions worldwide — suddenly have no income. They're not buying things. Not subscribing to Netflix. Not eating at restaurants. Not buying new clothes, new phones, new cars, new books, new anything.

Every consumer-facing company on earth just lost 30% of their customer base. Not because their product got worse, not because a competitor showed up, but because a third of the people who used to buy things simply cannot anymore.

Revenue drops. Stocks crash. Companies that were "safe" from AI — the ones making physical products, running restaurants, building houses — start laying off workers too, because *their* customers disappeared. The contraction feeds itself. The 30% becomes 40%.

And somewhere in the middle of this, you have a detail that economists might call "a humanitarian crisis" and I would call "billions of people who can't afford food."

What do billions of hungry, unemployed people with no prospects do?

They don't politely retrain. They don't quietly learn to code. They riot. They overthrow governments. They burn things down. This isn't speculation about human nature — it's the historical record. Every major revolution in history was preceded by exactly this: a large population that suddenly couldn't feed itself and had nothing left to lose.

## "The Government Will Figure It Out"

At this point, someone in the room always says it. "Sure, this sounds bad. But governments will step in. We'll figure out UBI, or retraining programs, or some kind of safety net. Humanity always adapts."

I want you to do something for me. I want you to take a moment and look around at all of the major governments and all of the major companies that you interact with on a daily basis. Look at Congress. Look at the EU. Look at your state legislature. Look at your health insurance company. Look at the DMV.

Now ask yourself: do you trust these institutions to solve the most complex economic transition in human history, in an efficient and timely manner, in a way that actually works out well for regular people?

Yeah. Me neither.

Our social safety nets — unemployment insurance, job retraining programs, community colleges — were designed for a world where economic transitions happen on the timescale of decades. Slow enough that a generation can age out while the next generation trains for the new reality. We're running a 50-year playbook on a 5-year timeline and wondering why the math doesn't work.

## The Final Rug Pull

Now, up to this point, I've been telling a story that has winners and losers. The people who are good at using AI keep their jobs. The people who aren't get displaced. It's brutal, but at least the skilled adapters survive.

I don't think that's where this ends.

Right now, the biggest input into AI is tokens — the computational units that power every AI interaction. Tokens cost money. Today, what's happening is that clever individuals are coming up with projects and using AI agents to build them. I described a bunch of these in [the companion essay](link-to-capabilities-essay). A person has an idea, they spend some money on tokens, and AI builds the thing. Individual creativity and initiative still matter.

But here's where it's going. Very soon — and by "very soon" I mean some of this is already happening — we're going to have agents whose entire job is to crawl forum posts across the internet and identify unsolved problems. Another layer of agents will evaluate which of those problems you could actually make money solving. Then implementation agents will build the solutions. Then review agents will check the work. Then deployment agents will ship it.

It's agents all the way down. No human in the loop except the one who paid for the tokens.

At that point, it stops being about who is *skilled* at using AI. It starts being about who has *capital* to run AI. Your individual creativity, your initiative, your ability to leverage these tools — none of that matters if someone with a million dollars can run a thousand times more agents than you.

Think about what that means. The first phase of AI disruption rewards the skilled individual — the clever person who figures out how to use the tools before everyone else. That's the phase we're in right now. But the second phase? The second phase rewards only capital. Your labor — even your *AI-augmented* labor — becomes irrelevant. The only thing that matters is how many tokens you can afford to burn.

This is the shift from a labor economy to a pure capital economy. And if you think the "30% unemployment" scenario sounded bad, imagine a world where human labor of *any kind* — skilled, unskilled, AI-augmented, creative, manual — is simply not the bottleneck anymore. Where the only thing that determines economic output is how much compute you can buy.

In that world, we are not the human who switched from horses to cars. We are not even the horse, who at least got to be a pet.

We are the hay.

## We Are Not the Human

Here's the thing I keep coming back to.

When people think about the car replacing the horse, they instinctively identify with the human in the story. *We're* the ones who invented the car. *We're* the ones who adapted. *We* went from riding horses to driving cars, and we were fine.

But the car didn't replace what the *human* did. It replaced what the *horse* did. The human was the one holding the reins. The human was the one who *chose* the better technology.

In the AI story, we are not the one holding the reins. We are not the one choosing to adopt a more efficient tool. We are the thing being replaced. We are the capability that a better technology is making obsolete.

The horses didn't retrain. There were no "new horse jobs." The economy didn't create exciting opportunities for horses in emerging fields. The population of working horses collapsed by 92%, and the ones that survived did so as pets and hobbies.

Now, I don't think the analogy is perfect — we have political agency that horses don't, and presumably we'll fight like hell for policies that protect us. But the economic forces are the same. When a cheaper, faster, more reliable technology can do everything you can do, the market does not care about your mortgage or your kids' college fund.

Jevon's Paradox says increasing efficiency creates new demand that absorbs displaced workers. And that's been true for every technology that made *humans* more efficient. But it was never true for the horses. Because the horses weren't being made more efficient. They were being *replaced*.

The question we should be asking ourselves is not "how will we adapt to AI?" It's "are we the human in this story, or are we the horse?"

I think we already know the answer. We just don't want to say it out loud.
